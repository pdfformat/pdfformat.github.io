About the PDF format

A PDF is designed to carry a document’s appearance with it. Here’s what that means in practice—and where the format has its limits.

  • What does PDF mean?
  • Why was PDF developed?
  • How does PDF preserve formatting?

  • Benefits and limitations

What does PDF mean?

PDF stands for Portable Document Format. A PDF is a type of digital file, usually ending in .pdf, that describes how a document should appear on a page. It can hold text, images, graphics, links and, in some cases, form fields or multimedia.

Think of a PDF as a finished presentation of a document rather than the working draft. You can learn about the parts inside it in Understanding PDF documents.

Why was PDF developed?

Adobe introduced PDF in the early 1990s to make it easier to exchange documents between different computers and software. The goal was to let a reader see a document without needing the application that created it. PDF later became an open international standard, maintained through the ISO process.

That portability matters when the sender and reader do not share the same fonts, operating system or editing software.

How does PDF preserve formatting?

Instead of asking a reader’s device to reflow a source document from scratch, a PDF describes page dimensions and the positions of text and graphics. Fonts may be embedded, so the reader does not need the same font installed. Images and vector graphics can be included alongside the text.

Consistency is a goal, not a guarantee: missing fonts, unusual features, damaged files or differences between viewers can still cause problems. Check the exported file using the PDF formatting guide.

Where are PDFs useful?

PDFs are common for invoices, forms, resumes, reports, manuals and documents intended for printing. They are also useful when a recipient needs to see a specific layout rather than edit the original file.

For example, sending a resume as a PDF can help preserve its spacing; keeping the editable source lets you update it later. Some organizations specify another format or a particular PDF standard, so follow their instructions first.

Benefits and limitations

Benefits

  • Predictable pages: layout usually travels well across devices.
  • Broad support: browsers and dedicated readers commonly open PDFs.
  • Mixed content: one file can include text, images and interactive fields.

Limitations

  • Editing can be awkward: complex changes are often easier in the source file.
  • Accessibility varies: a PDF needs appropriate tags, reading order and text alternatives; appearance alone is not enough.
  • Scans may be images: text may not be selectable until optical character recognition (OCR) is applied and checked.

PDF is useful for many handoffs, but it is not automatically secure or accessible. Choose the format that suits the reader and the task.
